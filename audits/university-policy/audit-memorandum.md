# Audit memorandum: academic decisions that rely on AI detection

Local audit for the AI Governance Initiative. Read on 2026-11-18. This file is the evidence trail for `evidence-brief.md` and `usac-resolution-draft.md`.

Themes: human oversight, AI reliability, transparency, privacy, and authorization. A detection score that is treated as a finding removes a person’s chance to contest a consequential classification, states a reliability the tool has not established, and, when the work is uploaded to an unapproved service, moves student coursework without a clear grant of permission.

No external message was sent. No student was interviewed. No detector was run. No paid API was called. Nothing in this file has been accepted by UCLA, USAC, UCSA, the Academic Senate, or any school board.

## 1. What already protects a UCLA student

These protections were read in the sources named. They are real, and the proposals below do not replace them.

**The conduct rule already covers unauthorized AI use.** The Interim 2026 UCLA Student Conduct Code, on the Office of Student Conduct site, defines cheating to include “unauthorized or prohibited use of artificial intelligence to compose or complete assignments,” and plagiarism to include use of artificial intelligence to compose work without attribution. Unless the faculty member specifies otherwise, submissions must be the student’s own work or must acknowledge the source. The page is titled interim. A separate effective-date line was not on the page that was read.

**The University bears the burden.** The Code’s standard of proof is preponderance of the evidence: “the University must prove that it is more likely than not that the student committed the misconduct.” If the information is insufficient under that standard, the matter is closed. Notice must state the conduct, the policy, and the right to an advisor chosen by the student or provided by the campus. The student may inspect relevant documents in the Office’s possession, with redactions required by law. The review is generally to conclude within 90 business days of the notice unless there is good cause.

**Separation cases get a hearing. Other cases get a limited appeal.** If the administrative resolution proposes suspension or dismissal and the student contests responsibility, the case goes to a hearing. The hearing decision is written, uses preponderance, and can be appealed. For a warning, probation, deferred suspension, or deferred dismissal, the appeal grounds are new information that would have materially affected the outcome, procedural error that materially affected the outcome, or a disproportionate sanction. Sanctions are deferred while a permitted appeal is pending. The Dean of Students decides non-separation appeals and is not limited to the original sanction. There is no appeal ground written as “the finding is not supported by a preponderance of the evidence.” A student can argue that failure to apply that standard is procedural error. The Code does not say so.

**Grades and discipline are already separated on paper.** Divisional Regulation A-306.3 says the instructor determines the grade from the student’s achievement. When plagiarism or other cheating is suspected, the instructor reports it, assigns the grade Deferred Report until proceedings are completed, and, if the proceedings find cheating, may then grade what is fairly the student’s own achievement. Regulation A-315 says a Deferred Report is also used when work is complete but the instructor cannot yet assign a grade, and that a Deferred Report is not calculated into the grade-point average. The grading-grievance process reviews a claim that a grade was assigned on a basis other than academic grounds. It does not regrade the quality of the work. A deadline announced for Fall 2026 runs through the last day of the next registered Fall, Winter, or Spring term, with an extension tied to a pending academic-integrity case.

**Systemwide law is general.** PACAOS section 102.01, in the interim text dated effective January 1, 2026, makes “all forms of academic misconduct including but not limited to cheating, fabrication, plagiarism, or facilitating academic dishonesty” a ground for discipline. It does not mention detection tools. Campus procedures implement that policy. The UCLA Code says amendments are made by the Chancellor in consultation with students, faculty, staff, and the Office of Student Conduct, and are submitted to the Office of the President before adoption. A UCSD-hosted copy of PACAOS 100 dated July 1, 2026 says proposed campus regulations go to the Office of the President, with the Office of the General Counsel, for consistency with the systemwide policies and the law. The official UCOP file was not downloaded, so that sentence is attributed only to the UCSD-hosted copy.

**UCSA has already spoken, on a different point.** The document linked from UCSA’s January 2025 list is titled “Resolution for Support on Strong Recommendation for AI Guidance in Syllabi,” dated January 2, 2025. It asks the systemwide Academic Senate to require syllabus language on permitted AI use, student representation on AI-policy committees, and campus training. It cites the UCLA Student Conduct Code as an integrity rule. It does not mention detection scores.

## 2. The gap

The gap is narrow. UCLA tells students that unauthorized AI use is misconduct and tells the University to prove it. UCLA does not say what a detection score is worth.

That omission matters in three places that were read:

1. **Sample teaching language is ahead of the Code.** UCLA Humanities Technology publishes sample syllabus language under which the instructor “may submit student writing to an AI writing detector” and “[a]ny confirmed use of AI writing tools will be treated as academic dishonesty.” “Confirmed” is not defined. The sample does not restate the report-and-Deferred-Report sequence. The page presents the paragraph as sample language adapted from teaching-center models. It is not a regulation. It is the most concrete campus text that treats a detector as a way to establish a violation.

2. **A grade penalty can bypass the hearing.** A hearing on responsibility is for proposed suspension or dismissal. A zero on one assignment, or a lowered course grade, is an instructor’s grade. Regulation A-306 says suspected cheating is reported and the grade waits. The regulation does not say what grade is entered when the student is found not responsible, and it does not say that a detection score is insufficient to “suspect” cheating. The grievance committee will not decide whether the work was good. A student whose paper was zeroed on a score, without a conduct case, has a textual argument that the grade was a penalty rather than an evaluation of achievement. Whether the committee would accept that argument is not established by any UCLA decision read for this audit.

3. **An appeal of a lesser conduct sanction cannot be taken simply because the student says the score is not enough.** The written grounds are new information, material procedural error, and disproportionate sanction. Failure to weigh contrary evidence can be described as procedural error, which is what the Nassau County court did to Adelphi under Adelphi’s own rules. UCLA’s Code does not require the reviewer to address a detection score, or the student’s drafts, in writing.

No UCLA or PACAOS sentence was found that authorizes a reviewer to treat a detection percentage as conclusive. The gap is the missing sentence, not a sentence that currently declares a score sufficient.

Digital & Technology Solutions’ “Guiding Principles for Responsible Use” tells users to keep a human in the loop and tells students that the Conduct Code applies to generative AI. It is not an evidentiary rule for discipline.

## 3. Comparable institutional choices

These are choices of other institutions, not evidence that UCLA has made the same choice.

- **Vanderbilt University**, Brightspace post, August 16, 2023. Vanderbilt disabled Turnitin’s AI detector. The post applies Turnitin’s then-stated 1 percent false-positive claim to Vanderbilt’s 75,000 Turnitin submissions in 2022 and says about 750 papers could have been incorrectly labeled. That figure is Vanderbilt’s arithmetic. It is not a count of observed false accusations at Vanderbilt. The current Vanderbilt generative-AI page still says the university does not offer an institutionally supported AI detector and points back to that decision.
- **Yale University**, Poorvu Center pages read through search extraction on 2026-11-18. Turnitin’s AI-detection feature is described as currently disabled. The Poorvu Center “does not currently support the use of AI detection tools.” A separate Poorvu page says the Center, with Yale’s AI Governance committee, is exploring agreements to enable detectors in Canvas, and that instructors may not submit student work to tools that have not passed Yale’s security review. The School of Public Health guidance discourages detection tools and gives reliability, harm, culture, and data-security reasons. Yale does not, on the pages read, have one university-wide rule that a score can never be used. The *Rignol* complaint alleges older website language that detection is not feasible. That allegation is not a current policy quotation.
- **A California charter school**, Sonoma Charter School Policy 0441, adopted October 13, 2026. Principle 2 states that AI detection software should not be the sole basis for disciplinary action or a grade penalty, and that misuse concerns rely on educator evaluation, alternative assessment, documented authorship evidence, and a rebuttal process. The policy cites Education Code section 33328.5 for the definition of artificial intelligence. One charter adoption does not establish statewide practice.

## 4. Disputes, separated by what they are

### 4.1 Newby: a judgment about Adelphi’s procedure

**Source opened:** the opinion text at Justia for 2026 NY Slip Op 26021, *Matter of Newby v. Adelphi University*, decided January 28, 2026, by Justice Randy Sue Marber. The opinion says Supreme Court, Nassau County, Index No. 615397/25. Justia’s header says “N.Y. Civ. Ct.” The header is not the opinion. Justia discloses that some of its metadata was produced with AI. The result below is taken from the ordered paragraphs and the court’s reasons.

**What the record, as recited by the court, contains.** Orion Newby submitted an essay on November 7, 2024, in a world-civilization course. The opinion recites, from the petition, that he had been diagnosed with Level 2 autism and was in Adelphi’s Bridges program, and that a Bridges tutor assisted him. The essay received a zero. Professor Micah Oelze filed a violation reporting a Turnitin “AI-generated score of 100%” and accusing use of Grammarly. Oelze’s report said Newby denied the essay was fabricated by AI and said he did use Grammarly. Newby’s written response categorically denied using AI to write the essay and pointed to Grammarly and ZeroGPT results, obtained by his parents, of a 0 percent chance the essay was AI-written. Academic Integrity Officer Michael Lacombe issued a determination that Oelze had found Newby responsible and sanctioned a plagiarism workshop, with a registration hold if the workshop was not completed. Oelze then emailed that he had thought Lacombe’s office would decide responsibility itself, and that the system instead defaulted the student to responsible unless he contested again. Newby’s appeal request said he had not had a fair hearing or an advisor. Lacombe denied the appeal, writing that a faculty group had found Oelze’s evidence met the school’s standards. The petition was filed July 18, 2025.

**Ruling.** The petition is granted. The violation and the denial of the appeal are annulled. Adelphi is directed to expunge the academic record of the violation. The sanction is rescinded. Adelphi’s motion to dismiss on documentary evidence is denied. Applications not specifically addressed are denied. The petition had also sought tuition and fee reimbursement. That relief is not in the ordered paragraphs.

**Reasons that are holdings about process.** Judicial review of a private college’s discipline is limited to whether the college acted arbitrarily or failed to follow its own rules. The court held that Oelze’s later email undercut the charge, so the violation and the denial lacked a valid basis. It held that the student-conduct bill of rights, including an advisor, applied and was not followed. It held that findings had to rest on a preponderance of the relevant information and a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and that Lacombe did not consider the two contrary detector results. It held that the official who issued the determination also decided the appeal, which defeated the appeal the academic-integrity code allowed.

**What the ruling is not.** It is not a finding that the essay was human-written. It is not a finding that Turnitin, Grammarly, or ZeroGPT is valid or invalid. The contrary scores are treated as evidence the university had to consider, not as proof the court accepted. A CBS account saying the court found that Newby did not use AI overstates the judgment. Vanderbilt Law School’s public comment on the case, read in search extraction, agrees that the court made no finding on detector reliability. That comment is secondary. The opinion is sufficient.

## 5. Studies, and why this audit does not add one

Use these only for the proposition that a score is not self-sufficient. Do not cite them as the error rate of a tool a campus uses today.

| Source | What was read | What it supports | What it does not support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turnitin AI-writing FAQ, updated 2026-11-03 | Vendor guide. In October 2026 Turnitin changed the English model. It says the indicator should not be the sole basis for action or a definitive grading measure. It says it tries to keep a false-positive rate under 1 percent for documents with more than 20 percent AI writing, checks that target on hundreds of thousands of pre-ChatGPT papers before a release, and does not display scores from 1 to 19 percent because those are less reliable. | The vendor of the tool named in *Newby*, the ACU reporting, and the *Kato* allegations tells institutions not to treat the percentage as a finding or as a grade. The October 2026 model change shows why a 2023 measurement is not a current rate. | An independent false-positive rate. A promise that a displayed score above 20 percent is true in a particular case. |
| OpenAI, classifier post, withdrawal as of 2023-07-20 | OpenAI’s page. On its English challenge set the classifier identified 26 percent of AI-written text as likely AI-written and labeled human-written text as AI-written 9 percent of the time. It was unreliable on short text, could be confidently wrong, and could be evaded by editing. | The maker of ChatGPT withdrew its own detector and said it was not fully reliable. | The error rate of Turnitin, GPTZero, or any other product. |
| Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu, and Zou, *Patterns* 4:100779 (2023), DOI 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779 | Published paper via DOI and PMC. Seven detectors. 91 TOEFL essays from a Chinese forum and 88 U.S. eighth-grade ASAP essays. Average false-positive rate 61.3 percent on the TOEFL set. Near-perfect accuracy on the U.S. eighth-grade set. Unanimous false flags on 19.8 percent of the TOEFL essays. Rewriting the TOEFL essays toward “native” word choice cut the average false-positive rate to 11.6 percent. A literary-language prompt dropped detection of ChatGPT text near zero. Authors caution against evaluative use, especially for non-native English writers. | A documented, uneven false-positive pattern in 2023 detectors, tied by the authors to low perplexity and limited lexical variety. | A result about Turnitin’s October 2026 model. The paper’s text that was read does not identify Turnitin as one of the seven. The TOEFL essays are from a forum, not from a controlled proctored sitting. The sample is small. A later review argues the literature does not show a universal non-native disadvantage. |
| Weber-Wulff and colleagues, *International Journal for Educational Integrity* (published 2023-12-25), DOI 10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z | Journal page. Fourteen tools, including Turnitin and PlagiarismCheck. Authors conclude the tools are neither accurate nor reliable. All scored below 80 percent accuracy, five above 70 percent. The dominant bias in their tests was classifying text as human-written. They estimate about 20 percent of AI-generated texts in the test would be misattributed to humans. Obfuscation made detection worse. Turnitin knew the documents were a test suite. | Independent of the vendor, a 2023 commercial detector was not reliable enough for the authors to recommend punitive use. False negatives and false positives both occurred. | A current false-positive rate. Their main error direction, missing AI text, does not cancel the separate false-positive evidence. Turnitin’s knowledge that it was being tested limits that one result. |
| Victor Angelier, *AI and Ethics* (2026-09-18), DOI page opened | A review. It restates Liang and Weber-Wulff and says later work does not support a universal non-native disadvantage. It also reports the author’s own same-day submission of one human-written manuscript to five detectors, with inconsistent labels. | A 2026 statement that the bias finding should not be universalized, and a reminder that cross-tool disagreement is easy to produce. | A false-positive rate. The one-manuscript check is exploratory. This audit does not rely on it for the proposed rule. |

**Recommendation.** Do not commission a statistical study, and do not run a local experiment, in order to justify the three provisions. The provisions say a score is not the whole case. Turnitin’s current instructions already say that. The published studies show that error rates large enough to matter have been measured, that they are not stable across tools or years, and that they can fall harder on some writing than others. A new bake-off would estimate one model on one corpus. Turnitin’s October 2026 architecture change is an example of why that estimate would not govern the next release. Testing current UCLA students would be human-subjects research and is outside this project’s authority. Calling a commercial detector at scale would risk a paid API call, which this project does not make.

A later technical project could reanalyze already-public pre-ChatGPT corpora with a locally installed, non-billed classifier, and publish the protocol. That would test a method. It would still not be a reason to wait before adopting a sole-evidence bar. It would also not be a contribution to a standards body until the method, the corpus, and the limits were fixed. This audit does not propose that experiment as part of the USAC resolution.

## 6. Candidate provisions

Each provision is a **proposal**. None is a claim that UCLA has already adopted it. Local identifiers are for this folder only. They have not been entered in `MASTER_TRACKER.md`.

### UP-001 — The score is not the sole basis

- **Document status:** proposed change to the UCLA Student Conduct Code and to Divisional Regulation A-306.
- **Exact sources of the gap:** Interim 2026 Code §§ 102.01a and 102.01c and the preponderance definition; Regulation A-306.3; PACAOS § 102.01. None of them states the evidentiary value of a detection output. HumTech sample syllabus language proceeds as if a “confirmed” detector result is a violation.
- **Text:** A generative-AI detection output shall not be the sole basis for a conduct finding or for a grade penalty imposed for suspected unauthorized generative-AI use. The preponderance standard is unchanged.
- **Who must act:** Academic Senate for the grading regulation. Chancellor, after consultation and Office of the President review, for the Code.
- **What an adopter does:** Tell reviewers and instructors, in the regulation they already use, that a percentage cannot close the case. Keep every other academic-integrity rule.
- **Not verified:** Whether any UCLA case file has in fact rested on a score alone. Practice was not audited.

### UP-002 — Notice, response, and a written weighing

- **Document status:** proposed change, implementing A-306 and the Code’s existing inspection and preponderance rules for this category of evidence.
- **Text:** Before a finding or a grade penalty based on suspected unauthorized generative-AI use, the student receives the work, the course rule, the tool name, the date, the output, and a chance to respond with drafts or other process evidence. The decision states the evidence other than the output and how the response was weighed. If the conduct proceeding does not find the misconduct, the work is graded without a penalty based on that suspicion.
- **Who must act:** Same as UP-001. The Senate sentence is the one that closes the grade path. The Code sentence is the one that closes the conduct path, including cases that never reach a hearing.
- **What an adopter does:** Add a short evidence paragraph to the notice and to the resolution letter. Do not require search-history production. The ACU reporting is the reason that requirement is rejected.
- **Not verified:** Whether current notice templates already attach detector reports. Templates were not collected.

### UP-003 — A prior course rule, and no unapproved upload

- **Document status:** proposed change to teaching guidance and, for the upload limit, to the Code or to a campus data rule.
- **Text:** A student is not penalized for generative-AI use that the instructor had not prohibited, in writing, before the work was undertaken. Coursework is not uploaded to a detection service the campus has not approved.
- **Relationship to existing text:** The Code already defaults to “unless otherwise specified by the faculty member” and already limits cheating to “unauthorized or prohibited” use. UP-003 states the timing of that specification and the data boundary the Code does not state. UCSA’s January 2025 resolution already asks for syllabus language. UP-003 does not repeat that request. It adds the timing and the upload limit.
- **Who must act:** Academic Senate and the teaching offices for the timing language. The Chancellor’s consultation process, or the campus unit that approves instructional software, for the upload limit. Yale’s Poorvu pages are an example of a security review, not a model UCLA has adopted.
- **Not verified:** Which detection services, if any, UCLA has licensed or enabled inside Bruin Learn. A campus tool inventory was not obtained.

Provisions that were considered and are not proposed: a ban on all detectors; a required numerical false-positive ceiling; a rule that a second detector’s 0 percent score proves innocence; a requirement that students disclose private browsing history; a USAC amendment of the Conduct Code. USAC’s bylaws limit a resolution to the opinion of the current Council on a matter that does not subject members’ welfare to substantial harm. The draft asks the bodies that hold the authority to consider text. It does not purport to enact it.

That shows a prior Council met on a Tuesday in Thanksgiving week. It does not show that the 2026 meeting will occur.

**Adding it at the meeting anyway.** The guide recommends against adding a resolution during the meeting if it missed the deadline. The bylaws allow an action item to be added when the agenda is approved only if waiting until the next meeting would materially harm its purpose, and additions require a two-thirds vote when a quorum is present. A request that the Senate and the Chancellor consider an evidence rule does not, on anything read here, become moot between November 24 and the next Tuesday. This draft is not written for the November 24 agenda.

**After passage, if it passes.** The main sponsors owe the Council two updates, at the second or third meeting and at the sixth or seventh, in a “Resolution Action Item Updates” section and orally. The Internal Vice President adds a special presentation if they miss those updates. Failure can cost a stipend check. The Council can cancel the updates by majority vote. The draft’s final clauses assign transmission to the primary sponsor so that the update duty has a defined act to report.

**Published names.** Bylaws Article VI, Section A.5.e forbids including an author or sponsor name in any published resolution. The guide ties a similar rule to paid publication of the resolution, including size, cost, and dates, and says contingency funding for that publication is a Council expense. The October 29, 2026 bylaws text that was extracted contains Section A.5.e and does not contain the guide’s advertisement-cost rules. Posted resolution PDFs on the USAC site do include sponsor names. The draft includes blank sponsor lines for the Council packet and flags Section A.5.e for any later public advertisement.

## 8. Path beyond USAC

**Academic Senate.** Regulation A-306 is a variance to systemwide Senate Regulation 780. An amendment is a divisional legislative act. The grading-grievance rules were amended June 12, 2025, which shows the Division does amend this regulation. The submission procedure for a divisional amendment was not separately retrieved. UP-001 and UP-002 should be offered to the Undergraduate Council as legislative text, not as a demand that the Council regrade any student.

**Office of the President.** A conduct-code sentence is a campus implementing regulation. It has to be consistent with PACAOS and is reviewed by the Office of the President before adoption, on the UCLA Code’s own amendment clause and on the July 1, 2026 PACAOS copy. A student-protective evidence rule does not contradict PACAOS 102.01, which is silent on detectors. That consistency judgment is the Office of the President’s, not this audit’s.

**UCSA.** The Board asks proponents to use its template. A resolution that amends UCSA bylaws must be emailed to the full Board, with the amendment text, at least one week before the vote. The three provisions do not amend UCSA bylaws. The vote threshold for an ordinary UCSA resolution was not found and is unverified. The January 2025 resolution should not be refiled. A later UCSA resolution, if one is wanted, should ask the systemwide Senate and the Office of the President for the evidentiary sentence only. USAC’s external vice president is the office that ordinarily carries undergraduate matters outward. This audit does not identify the current UCSA voting representative and does not instruct that office.

**School districts.** Education Code section 33328.5, as reproduced by california.public.law, requires the Superintendent’s working group to develop a model policy by July 1, 2026, including academic integrity and plagiarism. The section that was read requires development of a model. It does not require a district to adopt the model, and it does not mention detection software. The official legislative page returned 403. The California Department of Education model-policy URL presented a captcha and was not directly fetched. A search extraction of that URL contained a principle that detection software should not be the sole basis for discipline or a grade penalty. The same sentence appears in Sonoma Charter School Policy 0441, which was opened. District adoption is a local board act. *Kato* shows a dispute that ended without a ruling. It is a reason for a district to write the rule down. It is not a judicial order that any district do so. The reusable brief is the document to hand a board. The USAC resolution only expresses support for boards that adopt the limit.

**Standards projects.** The reusable sentence for a technical standard is the rule in the first paragraph of the evidence brief: an automated authorship classifier’s output is not sufficient evidence for a high-consequence decision about a person; the decision-maker records independent evidence and the person’s response. That sentence serves human oversight, reliability, transparency, privacy, and authorization. This audit did not read an OWASP, W3C, NIST, or ISO text and does not assert that any of those documents lacks the sentence. A later audit can test the sentence against a specific clause. It should not import Liang’s 61.3 percent, Turnitin’s 1 percent target, or any case caption into a standard as if it were a universal measurement.
