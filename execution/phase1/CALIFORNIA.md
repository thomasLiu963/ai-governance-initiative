# Track B — California rulemaking petition

Date of research: 2026-11-19. Strategy update the same day: this pathway is not dropped. The missing petitioner name and address block mailing. They are not evidence that a petition is the wrong instrument. The controlling list is `execution/phase1/MASTER_RANKING.md`.

This is a research memo, not a formal legal opinion.

One candidate survives, and it is a substantive citation amendment, not a formatting preference. It is labeled **DO NOW** for the legal case and blocked only on the petitioner's name, address, and signature. No petition was filed, no agency was contacted, and no account was created.

The candidate is a one-citation correction to the Civil Rights Council’s automated-decision system regulation, title 2, California Code of Regulations, section 11008.1(a). The sentence says the system concerns an “employment benefit” and then cites section 11008(i). In the regulation as adopted, subsection (h) defines “Employment Benefit” and subsection (i) defines “Employment Practice.” Those are different terms. The draft petition asks the Council to change the citation from 11008(i) to 11008(h). Draft text, marked DRAFT — NOT FILED, is in this memo and at `execution/phase1/drafts/california-petition.md`. The petitioner’s name and address are blank.

The California Privacy Protection Agency, the Department of Insurance, and the Department of Technology were checked. None has a second candidate that meets the bar. Reasons are below.

## What a 30-day response is

Government Code section 11340.7(a) requires the agency, within 30 days of receipt, to deny the petition in writing and state why, or to schedule the matter for a public hearing under the notice and hearing rules in Article 5 (commencing with section 11346). That 30-day act is a procedural decision. It is not approval of a regulation, not an Office of Administrative Law (OAL) filing, and not a change to the California Code of Regulations.

A grant still has to be carried out. If the Council opens a noticed rulemaking, Government Code section 11346.4(a) requires at least 45 days of notice before the hearing and the close of comments, and section 11346.4(b) gives the notice a life of one year. If the Council instead treats the citation fix as a change without regulatory effect, title 1, California Code of Regulations, section 100 gives OAL 30 working days to decide whether the filing qualifies, and the code changes only if OAL approves it and files it with the Secretary of State. Either path is after, and separate from, the 30-day petition response.

Both sections were added by Stats. 1994, Ch. 1039, and took effect January 1, 1995. The history notes on the official display do not show a later amendment.

### Section 11340.6

> Except where the right to petition for adoption of a regulation is restricted by statute to a designated group or where the form of procedure for such a petition is otherwise prescribed by statute, any interested person may petition a state agency requesting the adoption, amendment, or repeal of a regulation as provided in Article 5 (commencing with Section 11346). This petition shall state the following clearly and concisely:
>
> (a) The substance or nature of the regulation, amendment, or repeal requested.
>
> (b) The reason for the request.
>
> (c) Reference to the authority of the state agency to take the action requested.

### Section 11340.7

> (a) Upon receipt of a petition requesting the adoption, amendment, or repeal of a regulation pursuant to Article 5 (commencing with Section 11346), a state agency shall notify the petitioner in writing of the receipt and shall within 30 days deny the petition indicating why the agency has reached its decision on the merits of the petition in writing or schedule the matter for public hearing in accordance with the notice and hearing requirements of that article.
>
> (b) A state agency may grant or deny the petition in part, and may grant any other relief or take any other action as it may determine to be warranted by the petition and shall notify the petitioner in writing of this action.
>
> (c) Any interested person may request a reconsideration of any part or all of a decision of any agency on any petition submitted. The request shall be submitted in accordance with Section 11340.6 and include the reason or reasons why an agency should reconsider its previous decision no later than 60 days after the date of the decision involved. The agency’s reconsideration of any matter relating to a petition shall be subject to subdivision (a).
>
> (d) Any decision of a state agency denying in whole or in part or granting in whole or in part a petition requesting the adoption, amendment, or repeal of a regulation pursuant to Article 5 (commencing with Section 11346) shall be in writing and shall be transmitted to the Office of Administrative Law for publication in the California Regulatory Notice Register at the earliest practicable date. The decision shall identify the agency, the party submitting the petition, the provisions of the California Code of Regulations requested to be affected, reference to authority to take the action requested, the reasons supporting the agency determination, an agency contact person, and the right of interested persons to obtain a copy of the petition from the agency.

OAL’s own description of that duty, fetched 2026-11-19 from https://oal.ca.gov/faq/, matches the statute: written notice of receipt, a written denial with reasons within 30 calendar days, or, if the petition is not denied, scheduling the matter for a public hearing. OAL says a petition is a letter that states the change, the reason, and the agency’s rulemaking power. OAL has not adopted a regulation that implements section 11340.7. That is stated in OAL’s December 2022 decision, cited below.

Two related sentences matter for filing and for what “schedule a hearing” means.

Government Code section 11340.85(b)(5), from the same article display, fetched 2026-11-19:

> A petition regarding a regulation may be delivered to an agency by means of electronic communication if the agency has expressly indicated a willingness to receive a petition by means of electronic communication.

Government Code section 11346.4(b), fetched 2026-11-19 from https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11346.4.:

> The effective period of a notice issued pursuant to this section shall not exceed one year from the date thereof. If the adoption, amendment, or repeal of a regulation proposed in the notice is not completed and transmitted to the office within the period of one year, a notice of the proposed action shall again be issued pursuant to this article.

Section 11346.4(a) requires the notice at least 45 days before the hearing and the close of the public comment period.

Government Code section 11342.1, same article display, fetched 2026-11-19: except as provided in section 11342.4, the chapter does not confer or enlarge an agency’s authority. A regulation is effective only within the authority conferred and the standards prescribed by other law. Section 11342.2: a regulation is valid only if it is consistent and not in conflict with the statute and reasonably necessary to effectuate the purpose of the statute.

The text read for this memo is the Agency’s compilation “Effective 1/1/2026,” fetched 2026-11-19 from https://www.cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_statute_eff_20260101.pdf. Authority cited on the automated-decision sections is Civil Code section 1798.185.

Operative definitions and duties, quoted from that compilation:

Section 7001(e):

> “Automated decisionmaking technology” or “ADMT” means any technology that processes personal information and uses computation to replace human decisionmaking or substantially replace human decisionmaking.
>
> (1) For purposes of this definition, to “substantially replace human decisionmaking” means a business uses the technology’s output to make a decision without human involvement. Human involvement requires the human reviewer to:
>
> (A) Know how to interpret and use the technology’s output to make the decision;
>
> (B) Review and analyze the output of the technology, and any other information that is relevant to make or change the decision; and
>
> (C) Have the authority to make or change the decision based on their analysis in subsection (B).

Section 7001(ddd), opening sentence:

> “Significant decision” means a decision that results in the provision or denial of financial or lending services, housing, education enrollment or opportunities, employment or independent contracting opportunities or compensation, or healthcare services.

Section 7200:

> (a) A business that uses ADMT to make a significant decision concerning a consumer must comply with the requirements of this Article.
>
> (b) A business that uses ADMT for a significant decision prior to January 1, 2027, must be in compliance with the requirements of this Article no later than January 1, 2027. A business that uses ADMT on or after January 1, 2027, must be in compliance with the requirements of this Article any time it is using ADMT for a significant decision.

Section 7010(c) and (d) are in Article 2, not in Article 11. They require a pre-use notice, and an opt-out link except where section 7221(b) applies, for a business that “uses ADMT as set forth in section 7200, subsection (a).” Section 7011(e)(2)(F) and (G) likewise key the privacy-policy disclosure of the opt-out and access rights to section 7200(a).

That cross-reference is awkward. Section 7200(b) delays “the requirements of this Article” until January 1, 2027, for a business already using ADMT. Section 7010(c) cites only subsection (a). A reader can treat the privacy-policy and pre-use duties as due on the regulation’s effective date, January 1, 2026, or as delayed with the rest of the ADMT duties. That is a real drafting seam.

It does not survive. The seam closes on January 1, 2027, when subsection (b) requires compliance for existing uses and immediate compliance for new uses. A petition filed in November 2026 can produce a written denial or a hearing schedule inside 30 days. It cannot produce an amended regulation before January 1, 2027. After that date the two readings require the same conduct. A petition whose only defect expires before any amendment can take effect is not a sound use of this track.

Other CPPA text was read and does not supply a better defect:

- Section 7221(b)(1) is an exception to the opt-out when a human can hear an appeal after the decision. It does not say the appeal is “human involvement” under section 7001(e). The initial decision can still be ADMT. The appeal timeline is not a dangling citation: section 7021 is titled “Timelines for Responding to Requests to Delete, Requests to Correct, Requests to Know, Requests to Access ADMT, and Requests to Appeal ADMT,” and subsections (a) and (b) set a 10-business-day confirmation and a 45-calendar-day response, extendable by 45 days, for a request to appeal ADMT.
- Sections 7221(b)(2) and (b)(3) withhold the opt-out for specified admission, hiring, work-allocation, and compensation uses when the technology “works for the business’s purpose and does not unlawfully discriminate.” Those conditions can be applied with the underlying discrimination law. Adding a mandatory test or record would be a policy choice the Agency removed during this rulemaking. The final statement of reasons, fetched 2026-11-19 from https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_updates_cyber_risk_admt_fsor_and_uid.pdf, says the Agency deleted evaluation and human-involvement documentation requirements and left policies against unlawful discrimination as something a business may consider.
- Section 7157(b)(4) asks whether risk assessments involved the categories in Civil Code section 1798.140, subdivisions (v)(1)(A) through (L), (ae)(1)(A) through (G), and (ae)(2)(A) through (C). Those subdivisions exist. LegInfo, fetched 2026-11-19, https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.140., lists (v)(1)(L) as “Sensitive personal information,” (ae)(1)(A) through (G) (neural data is (ae)(1)(G)), and (ae)(2)(A) through (C). Section 7011’s use of (v)(1)(A) through (K) tracks the statute’s separate instruction, in the Agency’s January 1, 2026 statute compilation, to describe personal-information categories using (A) to (K) and sensitive personal information on its own list. Both regulatory sentences can be followed.
- Article 12, sections 7270 and 7271, states when an insurance company that is a “business” must comply with the CCPA: for personal information not subject to the Insurance Code and its regulations. That is a boundary rule. It is not an internal contradiction, and it is not a Department of Insurance regulation.

The cybersecurity-audit and risk-assessment articles are adopted and dated. They are long operational programs. No sentence in the text read for this memo is a contradiction, a missing step that makes a duty impossible, or a requirement that cannot be applied as written. Asking the Agency to add more human review, a shorter appeal, or a broader definition of ADMT would be a policy preference. Those proposals are rejected.

The release describes a December 15, 2026 workshop and a survey due December 30, 2026. It points back to a 2022 bulletin, not to a code section.

Bulletin 2022-5 is guidance. The NAIC’s compilation of artificial-intelligence bulletins, retrieved in search results on 2026-11-19 from https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/cmte-h-big-data-artificial-intelligence-wg-map-ai-model-bulletin.pdf, lists “California: Bulletin 2022-5 – Issued June 30, 2022” under guidance, and lists Colorado’s 3 CCR 702-10 as the contrasting regulation. A bulletin that was not adopted under the Administrative Procedure Act and filed with the Secretary of State is not a regulation. Government Code section 11340.5(a), from the article display fetched 2026-11-19, forbids an agency to enforce a bulletin that is a regulation as defined in section 11342.600 unless it has been adopted and filed. Petitioning the Department to “fix” Bulletin 2022-5 would ask it to amend a document that is not in the California Code of Regulations. That is the wrong instrument. If the legal theory were that the bulletin is an underground regulation, the remedy in section 11340.5 is an OAL determination, not a 11340.6 amendment of adopted text. This memo does not open that theory. There is no adopted regulatory sentence with a demonstrated defect.

The one insurance rule that does govern automated utilization review is a statute, not a Department regulation. Insurance Code section 10123.135(j), fetched 2026-11-19, requires a disability insurer’s artificial intelligence or other software tool used for utilization review to base determinations on the insured’s clinical information, not solely on a group dataset; not to supplant the provider’s decision; and not to deny, delay, or modify services based on medical necessity. Paragraph (2): “A determination of medical necessity shall be made only by a licensed physician or licensed health care professional competent to evaluate the specific clinical issues.” Paragraph (5), quoted in section 2 above, authorizes Department guidance and says that guidance is not subject to the Administrative Procedure Act. The Department’s rulemaking page confirms that, when the Department does adopt regulations, it uses the Administrative Procedure Act, including section 11340.6. The gap is the absence of an adopted artificial-intelligence regulation to amend. A petition asking the Department to invent a statewide underwriting code from a workshop record would be a policy proposal. It is rejected.

### 4.2 Operative language

### 4.3 Authority, and whether the petition process applies

Government Code section 12935(a), fetched 2026-11-19:

> The council shall have the following functions, powers, and duties:
>
> (a) To adopt, promulgate, amend, and rescind suitable rules, regulations, and standards that do either of the following:
>
> (1) Interpret, implement, and apply all provisions of this part, Article 9.5 (commencing with Section 11135) of Chapter 1 of Part 1 of Division 3 of Title 2 of this code, Sections 51, 51.5, 51.7, 54, 54.1, and 54.2 of the Civil Code, and Section 1197.5 of the Labor Code.

“This part” is the Fair Employment and Housing Act. The automated-decision rules interpret that part. The Council’s 2024 notice cites section 12935(a) as the authority for the rulemaking and says the action complies with the Administrative Procedure Act.

The requested action is an amendment of an adopted regulation of general application. It is the kind of request section 11340.6 describes. No statute found here restricts petitions to the Council to a designated group or prescribes a different petition form.

Section 11340.9 does not remove it. The Council is not a judicial or legislative body. The regulation is not a tax counsel ruling, a form, a rate, a traffic-control standard, or an order to a named person. It does not relate only to the internal management of the Department. It tells employers and other covered entities throughout the state which computational processes are automated-decision systems.

### 4.4 The problem

The definition gives two locations for the same term, and they are not the same term.

- The words say the computational process concerns an “employment benefit.”
- The citation says that term is “defined in section 11008(i).”
- Subsection (h) is the definition of “Employment Benefit.”
- Subsection (i) is the definition of “Employment Practice”: any act, omission, policy, or decision affecting an employment benefit or consideration for one.

A reader who follows the words asks whether the computation makes or facilitates a decision about hiring, promotion, compensation, discharge, or another benefit listed in subsection (h). A reader who follows the citation asks whether the computation concerns any act, omission, policy, or decision affecting a benefit or consideration for a benefit. The second reading is wider. It reaches policies and omissions, not only the benefit itself.

Later duties use the defined term. Section 11009(f) makes it unlawful to use an automated-decision system that discriminates on a protected basis, subject to available defenses, and it makes anti-bias testing relevant. Section 11013 requires preservation of automated-decision system data. Section 11016 applies the recruitment, inquiry, and screening rules to these systems, including the schedule-screening sentence in section 11016(c)(3)(A). Section 11017(e) applies the adverse-impact rule to a facially neutral automated-decision system. Those sections are only as definite as the definition.

This is not a request to cover more systems, or fewer. The sentence already chose “employment benefit.” The citation, left on the old letter after “Agent” was inserted as subsection (a), points at a different defined term. The regulation cannot be applied as written if both directions are followed. Section 11008’s preface, “unless the context otherwise requires,” lets a reader pick the words and ignore the letter. That is a reason the Council might deny the petition as unnecessary. It is also why the published text fails the clarity standard in Government Code section 11349(c), fetched 2026-11-19 from https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11349.: clarity means “written or displayed so that the meaning of regulations will be easily understood by those persons directly affected by them.” An applicant, an employer, and an agent should not have to decide which half of the definition wins.

The final text’s own renumbering marks are the evidence that the letter was not updated when the definitions moved. This is a contradiction inside the adopted rule, not a disagreement with the Council’s policy.

### 4.5 Replacement language

Amend section 11008.1(a) so the citation matches the term the sentence already uses. Change “section 11008(i)” to “section 11008(h).” Change nothing else. The full petition is in section 8 of this memo and in the draft file.

If the Council believes the citation was meant to incorporate “employment practice,” the petition asks for a written denial that says so. That reading would have to be reconciled with the words “employment benefit,” which the petition does not ask the Council to delete.

### 4.6 Why the change is substantive

The definition is the gate for every automated-decision duty in this employment chapter. Those duties are how California law currently allocates responsibility when a computation, rather than a person alone, makes or facilitates a decision about a job: who may be held responsible (section 11008(a) and section 11009), what records must be kept (section 11013), and when a screening tool that limits people by schedule or that measures bodies, voices, or faces has to be justified and accommodated (section 11016). A gate that cites the wrong definition does not give employers and workers one accountable rule.

The repair is small on the page and real in application. It makes the citation name the benefit the Council wrote into the sentence: hiring, promotion, compensation, discharge, and the other benefits in subsection (h). It does not ban a tool, require a new audit, or expand the Fair Employment and Housing Act. It makes the existing rule readable by the people who have to follow it. That is an accountability correction, and it is the clarity the Administrative Procedure Act already requires.

Title 1, California Code of Regulations, section 100(a)(4) treats “revising structure, syntax, cross-reference, grammar, or punctuation” as a change that can be filed without an Article 5 rulemaking when, and only when, the change “does not materially alter any requirement, right, responsibility, condition, prescription or other regulatory element.” Text fetched 2026-11-19 from https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/1-CCR-100, and the same clause appears in OAL’s November 2025 submission-text PDF at https://oal.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/166/2025/11/REMEDIATED-eSub-Portal-15-day-Notice-Text-Final.pdf. Cornell is not the official code printer. Westlaw timed out. The quoted clause is used because two independent public copies match. If OAL later decides that pointing the citation at subsection (h) would change who is covered, section 100 is unavailable and the Council would have to use Article 5. The petition asks for the citation correction and leaves that procedural choice to the Council and OAL. It does not ask for a broader definition.

### 4.7 Petition requirements, procedure, deadlines, exceptions

The petition must state, clearly and concisely, the three things in section 11340.6(a) through (c), quoted in section 1. OAL’s FAQ, fetched 2026-11-19, describes the petition as a letter containing those three things. There is no separate form.

On receipt, section 11340.7(a) requires written notice of receipt and, within 30 days, a written denial on the merits or a hearing scheduled under Article 5. Subdivision (b) allows a partial grant or denial and any other action the agency finds warranted, with written notice to the petitioner. Subdivision (c) allows reconsideration, requested in the form required by section 11340.6, no later than 60 days after the decision, and the reconsideration is itself subject to subdivision (a). Subdivision (d) requires the written decision to be sent to OAL for publication in the California Regulatory Notice Register at the earliest practicable date, with the identifications listed in that subdivision.

Electronic delivery is lawful only if the agency has expressly said it will receive a petition that way. Section 11340.85(b)(5), quoted in section 1. The Council’s October 19, 2026 release says written comments on a different, pending pregnancy-leave proposal may be emailed to council@calcivilrights.ca.gov: https://calcivilrights.ca.gov/2026/10/19/civil-rights-council-releases-proposed-regulations-to-modernize-pregnancy-leave-protections/ (fetched 2026-11-19). That sentence is about comments on a noticed proposal. It does not say the Council accepts a section 11340.6 petition by email. Mail is the method that does not depend on that unresolved point.

Addresses that appear in public Council documents:

- The 2024 automated-decision notice, fetched 2026-11-19, used Civil Rights Department, c/o the Assistant Chief Counsel then named in that notice, 555 12th Street, Suite 2050, Oakland, CA 94607, and Council@calcivilrights.ca.gov.
- A Council meeting notice for 2026 uses Civil Rights Department, Civil Rights Council, 651 Bannon Street, Suite 200, Sacramento, CA 95811: https://calcivilrights.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2025/04/Notice-and-Agenda-2026.10.05-CRC-Meeting-1.pdf (fetched 2026-11-19).
- A November 16, 2026 secondary account of the pregnancy-leave comment period names Suge Lee, Senior Legislative and Regulatory Counsel, at the Oakland suite. That name was not confirmed on an official Council page during this research. It is unverified.

No statute found here exempts this amendment from section 11340.6. Section 11340.9 does not. There is no fixed deadline by which a petition must be filed. The 30-day and 60-day periods run from receipt and from the decision, not from a calendar date on the books today.

OAL’s December 2022 decision, discussed below, is relevant to how a multi-member council can comply. OAL wrote that an agency that accepts a petition and proposes regulatory changes must schedule the public hearing in accordance with Article 5, and that a board or commission may not be able to decide whether to accept a petition without a meeting noticed under the Bagley-Keene Open Meeting Act. OAL denied a request to adopt a regulation stretching that 30-day schedule. The difficulty does not change section 11340.7(a). It does mean a grant, as opposed to a written denial or “other action” under subdivision (b), is the harder thing to finish inside 30 days.

### 4.9 The 30-day duty, stated from the text

Section 11340.7(a) requires two things. The agency must notify the petitioner in writing that the petition was received. Within 30 days, it must either deny the petition in writing and indicate why it reached that decision on the merits, or schedule the matter for a public hearing that complies with the notice and hearing requirements of Article 5.

Scheduling that hearing is not the adoption of the amendment. Article 5, including the 45-day notice in section 11346.4(a), still has to be completed, the Council still has to adopt the text, and OAL still has to approve it under section 11349.3 before it is filed and effective. Subdivision (b) also allows the agency to grant or deny in part, or to take other action it finds warranted, and to notify the petitioner in writing. A section 100 filing would be “other action” only if the Council and OAL treat the citation change as having no regulatory effect. This memo does not assume they will.

Subdivision (d) then requires the written grant or denial to be sent to OAL for the Notice Register. Publication is a disclosure of the decision. It is not the amendment.

## 5. Timing

All of the following is for the Civil Rights Council citation petition only.

**Time to produce a filing-ready petition.** The text is drafted. It is not filing-ready. The missing facts are the petitioner’s name, mailing address, and signature, and the date the petitioner signs. Section 11340.7(d) requires the agency’s published decision to identify “the party submitting the petition.” A letter without a named person is not a completed petition. Optional contact information (telephone or email) is not required by section 11340.6 and should be included only if the petitioner wants it. Whether mail should go to the Oakland suite, the Sacramento suite, or both is a practical gap, not a statutory one. The draft uses both published addresses and does not treat email as sufficient.

**Time to a first external response, from the evidence.** The statute requires written notice of receipt and, within 30 days, a written denial or a hearing scheduled under Article 5. The Board of Parole Hearings example ran from July 19, 2018 to a written denial dated August 1, 2018. CDCR issued a written denial on July 29, 2026; the receipt date for that petition is not in the published reconsideration notice. OAL’s 2022 decision records receipt on November 22, 2022 and a denial posted with the December 2022 file. The evidence-based expectation for a first written decision is inside the statutory 30 days, with publication in the Notice Register afterward “at the earliest practicable date,” which in the CDCR reconsideration was the September 11, 2026 register following a July 29 denial and an August 4 reconsideration request. That publication lag is not a promise.

**Time to acceptance or a regulation change.** Not known for a petition of this kind. The three dated petition decisions found were denials. They did not amend the code. The Council’s last full rulemaking on this subject ran from a May 17, 2024 notice to an October 1, 2025 effective date. A section 100 cross-reference change, if OAL accepts it, is reviewed within 30 working days of OAL’s receipt under title 1, section 100(c), and is filed with the Secretary of State only if OAL approves. No example was found of how long this Council takes to prepare a section 100 package after a petition. That interval is not estimated.

**Fixed procedural deadline.** None for filing the petition. Thirty days from receipt for the written denial or the scheduling of a hearing. Sixty days from the decision to request reconsideration. If a notice of proposed action is published, one year from that notice to transmit the completed action to OAL, under section 11346.4(b). At least 45 days of notice before the hearing and the close of comments, under section 11346.4(a).

**Independent verification.** Yes. A denial or grant must be written and transmitted to OAL for the California Regulatory Notice Register, section 11340.7(d). A resulting code change is verifiable by the Secretary of State filing and by the amended text of title 2, section 11008.1(a). A section 100 approval is verifiable by OAL’s filing under title 1, section 100(d).

**November 30, 2026.** Realistic for a filed petition if the petitioner’s name, address, and signature are supplied and the letter is mailed in time to be sent by that date. Not realistic for an agency decision. A petition mailed on November 30 is received after that date. The 30-day period runs from receipt.

**February 15, 2027.** Realistic for a written decision, a denial or a decision to schedule a hearing, if the petition is filed in November 2026 and the agency follows section 11340.7(a). The Parole Board and CDCR examples are written decisions on that kind of schedule, not code changes. Not realistic for implementation of an amended section 11008.1. An Article 5 proceeding cannot be noticed, commented, adopted, and approved by OAL in the weeks between a late-November filing and February 15. A section 100 filing could in principle be reviewed by OAL inside 30 working days after the Council submits it, but this research found no evidence that the Council would submit one by then. Implementation by February 15, 2027 is not claimed.

Amend section 11008.1(a) by replacing “section 11008(i)” with “section 11008(h).”

The subsection would read:

“Automated-Decision System.” A computational process that makes a decision or facilitates human decision making regarding an employment benefit, as defined in section 11008(h) of these regulations. An Automated-Decision System may be derived from and/or use artificial intelligence, machine-learning, algorithms, statistics, and/or other data processing techniques.

No other word in section 11008.1, and no other section, is requested to be changed.

2. Reason for the request

Section 11008.1(a) says an automated-decision system is a computational process that makes a decision or facilitates human decision making “regarding an employment benefit, as defined in section 11008(i).”

Section 11008(h) defines “Employment Benefit.” Section 11008(i) defines “Employment Practice.” “Employment Practice” is any act, omission, policy, or decision of an employer or other covered entity affecting any of an individual’s employment benefits or consideration for an employment benefit. That is a different term.

The citation points at the wrong subsection because a new subsection (a), “Agent,” was added to section 11008 in the automated-decision system rulemaking filed with the Secretary of State on June 27, 2025, and effective October 1, 2025. “Employment Benefit,” which had been subsection (g), became subsection (h). “Employment Practice,” which had been subsection (h), became subsection (i). Section 11008.1(a) kept the letter (i).

The words of section 11008.1(a) name an employment benefit. The citation names the subsection that now defines an employment practice. Both directions cannot be followed. Duties that use the term “automated-decision system,” including sections 11009(f), 11013, 11016, and 11017(e), depend on this definition.

The requested amendment makes the citation name the subsection that defines the term the sentence already uses. It does not add or remove a duty. If the Council concludes that the words “employment benefit” already control, this is a cross-reference correction. If the Council concludes that the citation was intended to incorporate “employment practice,” please deny the petition in writing and say so, because that reading conflicts with the words “employment benefit.”

3. Authority to take the action

Government Code section 12935(a) authorizes the Council to adopt, promulgate, amend, and rescind regulations that interpret, implement, and apply the Fair Employment and Housing Act. Section 11008.1 was adopted under that authority. Government Code section 11340.6 authorizes an interested person to petition for the amendment.
