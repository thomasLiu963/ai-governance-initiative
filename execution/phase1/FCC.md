Footnote 77 quotes the Joint Letter as suggesting that "the caller or sender inform[] the recipient of an option, including a separate call or message, to revoke consent with respect to all calls and text messages requiring consent." Footnote 84 quotes the proposed (a)(13): an affiliate, line of business, or division that obtains a separate consent and holds itself out to the called party throughout the calls and texts as that affiliate, rather than as the parent, "will be deemed a separate caller or text sender."

The three stages are not three separate questions the Commission wrote down. They are a definition of the outcome paragraphs 30 and 31 already time, the word "honored." Revised (a)(10) and (a)(11) require the request to be honored within ten business days and do not identify a permission record, cancellation of a communication the caller still controls, or a read of that record when a later communication is originated. Paragraphs (b)(3) and (d)(3) already require an immediate record for narrower classes of calls. That gap is real. It is the part of the draft that the Further Notice did not already answer by adopting the Report and Order.

**Stage 1, a permission record at receipt of the called number, the caller, and the scope.** This is the draft's answer to the premise of paragraphs 30 and 31, not a vote for seven days. Paragraph 31 asks whether automated exclusive means make processing fast enough to shorten or split the clock. A label is not the record. The draft says the ten-business-day maximum should remain and should measure work that remains after the record is written. The called-number field also answers the implementation half of paragraph 35: the record is for the number on which the request was received, and a record for any other number on an account is a separate record. The draft expressly does not answer whether 47 U.S.C. § 227 requires or forbids that second write. Paragraph 35 asks that legal question. This record does not resolve it. The scope field is the store that section II then fills. Stage 1 does not, by itself, answer paragraphs 32–34 or 37–38.

**Stage 2, cancellation of communications still in the caller's control.** No paragraph asks this in those words. It specifies what "honored" includes inside the time paragraphs 30 and 31 are measuring. The draft says the ten-day maximum limits work outside the caller's control, including a message already handed to a provider, and does not measure the cancellation of a message the caller still holds. That is responsive to paragraph 31's "any other issues relevant to this issue," because shortening the maximum, or shortening it only for exclusive-means callers, would leave this cancellation unstated. Stage 2 does not answer the seven-day cost question, the one-way question, the statutory "called party" question, or the affiliate question.

**Stage 3, a fresh permission check at send time.** Same placement. Paragraphs 30 and 31 treat processing speed as one fact. A clearance stored before the revocation, and reused when the message is sent, is a different fact from a read at origination. The draft uses that read in two further answers. On paragraph 31's split-clock question, a designated exclusive means honors a request only when it performs all three operations, so the maximum does not differ according to whether an exclusive means was designated. On paragraphs 32–34, a reply path or a disclosed one-way alternative honors a revocation only when it performs the three operations, including the send-time read. The existence of the path does not itself honor the request. Stage 3 also makes "silence does not restore consent" operational: a later origination cannot reuse a clearance written before the revocation. The decision not to treat silence as a new revocation is a decision not to write, which is stage 1 and section II, not the send-time read alone. Paragraph 36 asks whether treating silence as a revocation of every future robocall reflects the customer's intent. The draft answers that question: failure to respond does not revoke consent and does not restore it.

**Section II's same-interaction condition is a direct answer to paragraphs 35 and 36, and it is not one of the three stages.** A category-limited informational revocation is available only when the communication discloses the limit and the same interaction provides an automated method that records, for that called number and that caller, a revocation of every communication for which consent is required. If either condition is absent, the record covers every such communication from that caller to that number. An utterance that states a narrower scope is recorded with the scope it states. That answers paragraph 35's question whether the limited reading should depend on an all-categories method, whether the method must be automated, and what form it should take. It rejects footnote 77's "separate call or message," because a later communication is unavailable when the consumer does not answer it. It does not specify keypress versus keyword versus a webpage control beyond "automated" and "same interaction." Paragraph 39's confirmation questions are answered by not requesting a confirmation call, and by providing that a confirmation states the scope already recorded, does not postpone the record, and does not continue a revoked scope.

**Section III's affiliate sentence is a conditional answer to paragraphs 37 and 38, and it is narrower than the questions.** The draft does not recommend modifying (d)(5), extending it to every robocall, or adopting proposed (a)(13). If the Commission specifies affiliate coverage, the specification should name the stored fields the origination path checks: the caller identity disclosed when consent was obtained, the caller identity disclosed in the communication being revoked, and any affiliate, line of business, or division that disclosure names as covered. A name substituted at later origination is not one of those fields. Paragraph (d)(5) continues to govern the requests it already governs. That is an answer to "what form of guidance," not an adoption of the reasonable-expectation standard for every robocall and not an adoption of footnote 84.

The three stages genuinely add a definition the adopted rule text does not contain, and they answer the timeframe questions by saying what the existing maximum measures. They do not supply the cost, benefit, or prevalence numbers paragraphs 31, 34, 35, 41, and the IRFA ask for. The draft says so.

## Whether the restraint is right

The draft keeps the ten-business-day maximum. It does not recommend seven business days, a one-way ban, an extension of (d)(5), or footnote 84's affiliate paragraph. That restraint matches the questions, because the questions ask for evidence this record does not have, and because a broader recommendation would not perform the operations the draft is actually proposing.

- **Seven days.** Paragraphs 30 and 31 ask whether seven business days is reasonable and what it would cost, including for smaller entities. The draft has no cost study. Answering "do not adopt seven, because this record does not show that seven is reasonable or unreasonable, and a shorter maximum would not add the record, the cancellation, or the send-time read" is a complete answer. Recommending seven would be a number without the measurement the question asks for.
- **A different maximum for exclusive-means callers.** Paragraph 31 asks this directly, and asks whether two clocks would confuse consumers. The draft answers no. The call does not disclose which clock applies. The section already uses "immediately," six business days, and ten business days for different exemptions. A further split would reward a designated intake path without requiring that path to cancel a controlled message or to read the record at send time.
- **A one-way ban.** Paragraphs 32–34 ask for the extent of one-way use and for the cost of removing it, and they specifically ask about informational messages and schools. The draft has no count and no cost measurement, so it does not recommend eliminating one-way protocols. It answers the school and informational question that was asked: a one-way informational communication, including one sent by or on behalf of a school or another public institution, may continue when each message discloses an alternative that performs the three operations. A ban would not be better supported by this record.
- **Extending (d)(5), or adopting footnote 84.** Paragraphs 37 and 38 ask whether to modify, specify, or extend the reasonable-expectation standard, and they put the joint letter's (a)(13) on the table. An origination path checks a stored key. It does not observe an expectation. Extending (d)(5) to every robocall would extend a standard this fixture cannot apply. Footnote 84 is already before the Commission. Repeating it would not add the stored-field check. The draft's conditional sentence is the part that answers "what form of guidance" with something the send path can look up.

Other comments filed since October 26 urge shorter clocks: one business day, 24 hours, seven business days, and five calendar days. Those filings show that a shorter number is a live answer other people are giving. They do not supply the missing cost measurement for this draft. Keeping ten days remains the answer this record can support.
