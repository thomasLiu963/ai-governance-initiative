## Three-stage mechanism

The adopted paragraphs (a)(10) and (a)(11), read today in the Federal Register final rule, require a revocation made in the manner those paragraphs describe to be honored within a reasonable time not to exceed ten business days from receipt. They do not define honoring. The revised (a)(10) text does not contain the pre-amendment sentence that a listed-method revocation “is considered definitively revoked and the caller may not send additional robocalls and robotexts.” The revised (a)(12) permits one confirmation text with no marketing or promotional information and a five-minute presumption. It does not contain the pre-amendment limits that the confirmation be the only additional message and that the sender stop every consent-based text until the recipient clarifies the scope.

The public text asks the Commission to treat a request as honored only when all three of the following have occurred:

1. **Permission record.** At receipt, the caller records the called number on which the request arrived, the caller or sender, and the scope. A channel note limits that scope only when the request itself states a channel limit.
2. **Queue cancellation.** The caller cancels each automated communication that the recorded scope covers and that is still within the caller’s control.
3. **Send-time check.** After the record is written, the caller originates no automated communication inside that scope other than a confirmation paragraph (a)(12) permits, and a later origination reads the permission state at the moment of sending.

A keyword label and an acknowledgement are not substitutes for those three operations. The ten business days measure work that remains after the record is written and that the caller no longer controls, including a message already handed to a provider. They are not a grace period for new originations or for leaving a controllable queued message in place. The maximum does not change because the caller designated an exclusive means. An exclusive means honors the request when it performs the same three operations.

That definition is the comment’s answer to paragraphs 30 and 31. Seven business days, or a shorter maximum only for exclusive-means callers, would still leave the outcome “honored” undefined. This record has no cost study, so the comment does not find seven business days reasonable or unreasonable.

## I. Timeframe for honoring a revocation request

Paragraphs 30 and 31 ask whether to reduce the maximum time for honoring a revocation. The Joint Letter suggests seven business days unless the caller shows that a longer time is reasonable. The paragraphs also ask whether exclusive-means callers should have a different maximum.

Revised paragraph (a)(10), in Appendix A of FCC 26-67 and in the Federal Register final rule, requires a request made in the manner described there to be honored within a reasonable time not to exceed ten business days from receipt. Revised paragraph (a)(11) states the same maximum for callers that designate no exclusive means. The revised paragraph (a)(10) does not contain the sentence, still displayed in the unofficial eCFR text of 47 CFR 64.1200 on November 18, 2026, and quoted in paragraph 3 of FCC 26-67 as part of the 2024 order, that consent revoked by a listed method “is considered definitively revoked and the caller may not send additional robocalls and robotexts.” From November 25, 2026, the timing language in the revised paragraphs is the duty to honor the request within ten business days. That duty names an outcome. It does not say whether the caller has written a permission record, cancelled a communication still in the caller’s control, or read the record at send time. A label and an acknowledgement can both be complete while a later origination uses a clearance stored before the revocation. Paragraph (b)(3) already requires an automatic do-not-call entry and immediate termination for a specified call. Paragraph (d)(3) already requires a covered residential request to be recorded when it is made and still allows ten business days to honor it. Revised paragraphs (a)(10) and (a)(11) contain neither operation.

Seven business days, or a shorter maximum only for exclusive-means callers, would leave that gap in place. Section 64.1200 already uses “immediately” for the financial-institution and healthcare opt-outs, six business days for package-delivery opt-outs, and ten business days in paragraphs (a)(10) and (d)(3). A further split would be a distinction the call itself does not disclose. This record contains no cost study and supplies no basis for finding that seven business days is reasonable or unreasonable for any class of caller.

The Commission should amend paragraphs (a)(10) and (a)(11) so that a caller honors a revocation request only when all three of the following have occurred.

1. At the time of receipt, the caller records a permission state identifying the called number on which the request was received, the caller or sender, and the scope. The record may note the arrival channel. That note limits the scope only when the request states a channel limit.

2. The caller cancels each automated communication that the recorded scope covers and that remains within the caller’s control.

3. After the record is written, the caller originates no automated communication within the recorded scope other than a confirmation that paragraph (a)(12) permits, and any later origination decision reads the permission state at the moment of sending.

An automated label and an acknowledgement do not honor the request. The ten-business-day maximum limits work that remains after the record is written and that is outside the caller’s control, including a message already handed to a provider. During those ten days the caller still may not originate a new communication inside the recorded scope, and a controllable queued communication still has to be cancelled. The maximum does not depend on whether the caller designated an exclusive means. A designated exclusive means honors a request only when it performs the three operations. These comments do not ask the Commission to require processing of a request that arrives by some other method.

A local specification fixture on fixed strings shows that split and nothing more. “Stop” can be labeled as a standardized word while a later message is still sent, because the label does not write the store. A message flagged as still in the caller’s control can be cancelled while a message flagged as already handed off stays pending. A clearance stored before the write allows a send that a send-time read suppresses, and the empty string leaves the store unchanged. The fixture called no model, network service, or paid API, and it observed no carrier, provider, dialer, handset, or person. A passing check means the local objects matched that case. It is not evidence of cost, prevalence, or intent.

## II. Category-limited revocation, silence, and the called number

Paragraphs 35 and 36 ask whether a category-limited reading should be available only when the caller provides a method to revoke consent for every message type that requires consent, whether that method must be automated, whether a revocation stops at the telephone number rather than every number on an account, and whether silence after a clarification should revoke every future robocall. Paragraph 39 asks about a confirmation call or text.

The standardized words in paragraph (a)(10)(ii) identify a revocation. They do not name a category, an account, or an affiliate. Revised paragraph (a)(10) lets the caller read an informational revocation as covering the category of the prompting communication. That category is the caller’s label. Where the stored categories are finer than the choice disclosed to the consumer, “stop” writes the caller’s taxonomy. Footnote 77 describes the Joint Letter’s suggestion of an all-categories option “including a separate call or message.” A later communication is unavailable when the consumer does not answer it. No reply is the same observable event for a non-delivery, an unread message, and a decision to stand on the revocation already made. It has no content from which a new scope can be read.

Revised paragraph (a)(12) permits a confirmation text that confirms the request and contains no marketing or promotional information, with a five-minute presumption. It does not contain the pre-amendment requirements, displayed on eCFR on November 18, 2026, that the confirmation be the only additional message and that the sender stop every consent-based text until the recipient clarified the scope. Silence during a clarification wait should not be stored as a new revocation or as consent to resume.

The Commission should provide that a caller may record an informational revocation as limited to the category of that communication only when the communication discloses the limit clearly and conspicuously and the same interaction provides an automated method that records, for that called number and that caller, a revocation of every communication for which consent is required. If either condition is absent, the caller records the revocation as covering every such communication from that caller to that called number. An utterance that states a narrower scope is recorded with the scope it states. Failure to respond to a later communication does not revoke consent and does not restore it. A confirmation permitted by paragraph (a)(12) states the scope already recorded. It does not postpone the record and does not continue a revoked scope. These comments do not request a confirmation call. If the Commission permits one, it is a single call, it states the recorded scope, and it contains no advertising or telemarketing.

The permission state names the called number on which the request was received. A record for any other number on an account is a separate record. These comments do not answer whether 47 U.S.C. § 227 requires or forbids that second write.

Paragraph 39 also notes proposals to shorten the standardized word list and to delete the rebuttable presumption in paragraph (a)(11). This record does not measure either change. If the Commission adopts either change, every method the caller remains required to accept honors the request by the three operations in section I.

## III. Reply paths and affiliate identity

Paragraphs 32 through 34 ask whether to remove the one-way texting provision, how widely one-way protocols are used, and whether one-way texting should remain for informational messages and for public institutions such as schools. Paragraphs 37 and 38 ask whether to modify, specify, or extend paragraph (d)(5).

The one-way provision already requires disclosure that two-way texting is unavailable and a reasonable alternative. A required reply string would be another intake path. Either path honors a revocation only when it performs the three operations in section I. This record contains no count of one-way protocols and no measurement of the cost of removing them. A one-way protocol may be used for an informational communication, including a communication sent by or on behalf of a school or another public institution, when each message discloses an alternative that performs the three operations.

Paragraph (d)(5) states a reasonable-expectation standard for the residential do-not-call requests it governs. An origination path checks a stored key. It does not observe an expectation. These comments do not recommend modifying paragraph (d)(5), extending it to every robocall, or adopting proposed paragraph (a)(13). If the Commission specifies affiliate coverage for a consent revocation, the specification should name the stored fields the origination path checks: the caller identity disclosed when consent was obtained, the caller identity disclosed in the communication being revoked, and any affiliate, line of business, or division that disclosure names as covered. A name substituted at later origination is not one of those fields. Paragraph (d)(5) continues to govern the requests it already governs.
